Hearing the Shape of a Function

How orthogonality turns an arbitrary periodic signal into a collection of simple oscillations.

November 18, 20263 minute readAnalysis · Fourier series

At first glance, a jagged periodic graph and a perfectly smooth sine wave appear to belong to different worlds. Fourier’s remarkable insight was that the former can be assembled from the latter—provided we learn to think of functions as vectors.

The surprising proposal

A periodic function need not be smooth. It may have corners, sudden jumps, or long stretches in which almost nothing happens. Yet a vast class of these functions can be represented, in an appropriate sense, as a sum of waves:

f(x)∼a02+∑n=1∞(ancosnx+bnsinnx). f(x) \sim \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(a_n\cos nx+b_n\sin nx\bigr).

The symbol ∼\sim is deliberate: the meaning of convergence depends on our assumptions and on where we evaluate the series. This qualification is part of the beauty of the theory, not an inconvenience.

A frequency of nn means that the corresponding wave completes nn cycles over an interval of length 2π2\pi. Increasing nn produces finer and finer oscillations. The coefficients determine how strongly each oscillation contributes.

Orthogonality is the key

The trick is to view functions as points in a vector space. On an interval of length 2π2\pi, define the inner product

⟨f,g⟩=1π∫−ππf(x)g(x)dx. \langle f,g\rangle = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)g(x)\,dx.

For positive integers mm and nn, trigonometric orthogonality gives

∫−ππcos(mx)cos(nx)dx={π,m=n,0,m≠n. \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx=\begin{cases} \pi,&m=n,\\ 0,&m\ne n. \end{cases}

The same pattern holds for sine functions, while sine and cosine are mutually orthogonal. In ordinary Euclidean geometry, orthogonal vectors can be separated by taking dot products. Here, we can separate frequencies by integrating.

Multiplying the proposed series by cos(mx)\cos(mx) and integrating isolates a single coefficient:

am=1π∫−ππf(x)cos(mx)dx. a_m=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\cos(mx)\,dx.

(1)

Analogously,

bm=1π∫−ππf(x)sin(mx)dx. b_m=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\sin(mx)\,dx.

Equation equation (1) is not a lucky formula to memorize. It is a projection: the amount of ff pointing in the direction of one particular wave.

An example with a sharp edge

Consider the odd, 2π2\pi-periodic function that agrees with f(x)=xf(x)=x for −π<x<π-\pi<x<\pi. Oddness immediately forces every cosine coefficient to vanish. Integration by parts yields

bn=2π∫0πxsin(nx)dx=2(−1)n+1n. b_n=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi x\sin(nx)\,dx =\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}.

Thus the Fourier series is

x∼2(sinx−sin2x2+sin3x3−⋯). x\sim 2\left(\sin x-\frac{\sin 2x}{2}+\frac{\sin 3x}{3}-\cdots\right).

Although the original periodic function jumps at odd multiples of π\pi, every finite partial sum is smooth. The approximation becomes increasingly accurate away from the jump. Near the discontinuity, it exhibits the characteristic overshoot known as the Gibbs phenomenon.

What convergence means

There are several distinct questions we might ask. Does the series converge at every point? Does it converge uniformly? Does the error become small on average, even if it behaves poorly at a few points?

The last question leads to the natural setting of square-integrable functions. In L2[−π,π]L^2[-\pi,\pi], Fourier partial sums converge to the function in mean square. One consequence is the celebrated Parseval identity:

1π∫−ππ|f(x)|2dx=a022+∑n=1∞(an2+bn2). \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(x)|^2\,dx =\frac{a_0^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2+b_n^2).

(2)

The left side measures a function’s total squared magnitude; the right side measures the same quantity frequency by frequency. Energy is conserved under the change of coordinates.

For square-integrable 2π2\pi-periodic functions, the trigonometric Fourier coefficients determine the function uniquely up to equality almost everywhere.

This is a profound kind of completeness. The collection of all sine and cosine waves is large enough to describe any L2L^2 periodic signal, up to the natural notion of equality in that space. See (Stein and Shakarchi 2003) for a systematic development.

A different kind of seeing

Fourier analysis replaces the question “Where is the function large?” with “Which frequencies does it contain?” Neither description is more real than the other. The usefulness comes from moving between them.

In the language of linear algebra, we have changed basis. In the language of physics, we have separated a signal into modes. In the language of mathematics, we have found structure inside complexity.
