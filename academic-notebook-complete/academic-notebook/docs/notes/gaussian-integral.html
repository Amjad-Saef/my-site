The Gaussian Integral, Squared

A one-dimensional integral becomes simple when we turn it into an area calculation.

August 30, 20262 minute readCalculus · Notes

The Gaussian integral is famous because its antiderivative cannot be expressed using elementary functions, yet its integral over the entire real line has an exact answer.

The obstacle

We want to compute

I=∫−∞∞e−x2dx. I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx.

Trying to find an elementary antiderivative is not the right route. Instead, observe that the integrand is positive, so I>0I>0, and square the integral:

I2=(∫−∞∞e−x2dx)(∫−∞∞e−y2dy). I^2=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx\right) \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-y^2}\,dy\right).

Tonelli’s theorem allows us to combine the two integrals:

I2=∬ℝ2e−(x2+y2)dxdy. I^2=\iint_{\mathbb R^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy.

Switch to polar coordinates

In polar coordinates, x2+y2=r2x^2+y^2=r^2 and dxdy=rdrdθdx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta, so

I2=∫02π∫0∞e−r2rdrdθ=2π[−12e−r2]0∞=π. \begin{aligned} I^2&=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta\\ &=2\pi\left[-\frac12 e^{-r^2}\right]_{0}^{\infty}\\ &=\pi. \end{aligned}

Taking the positive square root yields

∫−∞∞e−x2dx=π. \boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx=\sqrt\pi}.

The useful lesson

When one-dimensional integration becomes difficult, increasing the dimension can reveal symmetry that was invisible before. Here, a product of identical integrals creates a rotationally symmetric problem—and symmetry does most of the work.
