A Short Route to Euler's Identity

Why exponentials can encode rotations, and how five constants fall into a single equation.

September 24, 20262 minute readComplex analysis · Notes

The formula eiπ+1=0e^{i\pi}+1=0 looks almost ceremonial. Its derivation, however, is a straightforward and exceptionally useful consequence of the power-series definition of the exponential function.

Start with a power series

For every complex number zz, the exponential function is defined by the absolutely convergent series

ez=∑n=0∞znn!. e^z=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{n!}.

Put z=ixz=ix for real xx. Because powers of ii repeat in a cycle of length four, we can group even and odd powers:

eix=(1−x22!+x44!−⋯)+i(x−x33!+x55!−⋯). \begin{aligned} e^{ix} &=\left(1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\cdots\right)\\ &\quad+i\left(x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots\right). \end{aligned}

The real and imaginary parts are precisely the Taylor series of cosine and sine. Thus

eix=cosx+isinx. e^{ix}=\cos x+i\sin x.

(1)

Evaluate at a half-turn

A half-turn corresponds to x=πx=\pi. Since cosπ=−1\cos\pi=-1 and sinπ=0\sin\pi=0, equation equation (1) becomes

eiπ=−1, e^{i\pi}=-1,

or, in its most familiar form,

eiπ+1=0. \boxed{e^{i\pi}+1=0}.

The geometrical meaning

For real xx, the complex number eixe^{ix} lies on the unit circle. Varying xx traces a rotation. Exponentials, trigonometry, and complex multiplication turn out to be different descriptions of the same structure.
